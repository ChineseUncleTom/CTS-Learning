Lv.1 · sound waves

Squeeze the air 〰️

Sound is waves of compression moving through a compressible medium. Something vibrates, shoves the air next to it, and that shove travels. The air itself doesn't fly across the room — the squeeze does.

Drop a rock in a pond ⟶ rings travel out, water stays put. Sound is that, but spherical and made of squeezes. And in space? Nothing to squeeze. That's why nobody can hear you scream. 🚀
★ SKILL GET! Wavelength is a ruler. Frequency is a clock.

Wavelength = the distance between two points one full cycle apart — measured in metres. Frequency = how many cycles happen per second — measured in hertz. One is space, one is time, and they're locked together by the speed of sound.

wavelength = speed ÷ frequency · λ = 343 / f speed of sound in air at 20 °C / 68 °F, at sea level = 343 m/s (1,125 ft/s)
🤓 nerd bait 343 = 7 × 7 × 7. The speed of sound is a perfect cube. You will never forget it again, and it's the number every wavelength question hangs off.

🎹Why we count in octaves.

👂

Your ear responds to frequency logarithmically. 220→440 Hz sounds like the same jump as 440→880 Hz, even though one adds 220 Hz and the other adds 440 Hz.

8️⃣

So we band the spectrum by doubling, and call each band an octave — from octavus, Latin for eight, because the Western scale splits each band into eight tones.

🔟

Human hearing (20 Hz – 20 kHz) divides into 10 octave bands, each named by its centre frequency. Graphic EQs slice it finer still — into ⅓-octave bands.

★ SKILL GET! Low and long. High and short.

Wavelength and frequency are inversely proportional. The lowest frequencies have the longest wavelengths (20 Hz = 17.2 m, longer than a bus) and the highest have the shortest (20 kHz = 17.2 mm, thinner than your thumb).

Harmonics — why a guitar isn't a banjo 🪕

🎸

Pluck a string tuned to A = 220 Hz and it doesn't only make 220 Hz. It also rings at 440, 660, 880, 1100, 1320… — whole-number multiples of the fundamental. Those are its harmonics.

🎻

The relative proportions of those harmonics are what make a note sound like a guitar rather than a mandolin, ukulele, balalaika, double-bass or banjo. That character is called timbre.

⚠️

The trap: the fundamental IS the first harmonic. 220 Hz is harmonic #1, 440 Hz is #2, 660 Hz is #3. Not "the fundamental plus the harmonics".

🌀 bonus vocabulary Every complex sound can be broken into a stack of simple sine waves. The maths that does it is Fourier analysis, and its output is the Fourier series. It sits at the heart of every DSP application you'll meet later in this chapter.
Lv.2 · logarithms & the decibel

The unit that isn't a unit 📊

Your ears cover a range of about 3,000,000 : 1 between the quietest sound you can detect and the point it starts to hurt. Writing that on a linear scale is absurd — so audio runs on logarithms.

🪜What a logarithm actually is.

🔢

The log of a number is how many times 10 must be multiplied by itself to get there. log(1,000) = 3, because 10×10×10. log(100,000,000) = 8. log(0.0001) = −4.

📏

On a normal ruler, every mark adds one. On a log ruler, every mark multiplies by ten. That's how you fit a range of millions onto one page.

🧠

And it isn't just hearing — the Weber–Fechner law says all our senses work this way. Sensation is proportional to the log of the stimulus.

★ SKILL GET! Power gets Ten. Volts and Distance get Twenty.

A decibel is a logarithmic ratio between two quantities of the same property. Power compares directly, so dB = 10 log(P1/P2). But voltage, current and distance all get squared on their way to power — so they need dB = 20 log(V1/V2). Same for 20 log(D1/D2).

dB = 10 × log(P1 / P2) · power
dB = 20 × log(V1 / V2) · voltage, current, distance Bel = log(q1/q2), named for Alexander Graham Bell — but the Bel is too big, so 1 dB = 0.1 Bel
"It's 85 dB." …85 dB compared to what? A decibel is a comparison, not an absolute. Saying "it's 10 degrees hotter today" tells you nothing unless you know yesterday. 🌡️
the big one

0 dB SPL

The threshold of human hearing — a pressure of 20 µPa. Every SPL figure you ever see is referenced to this.

voltage refs

0 dBu & 0 dBV

0 dBu = 775 mV · 0 dBV = 1.0 V. And −20 dBu isn't a negative voltage — it's just a voltage less than 775 mV.

power ref

0 dBm

0 dBm = 1 mW of electrical power.

★ SKILL GET! One barely. Three clearly. Ten doubly.

The three numbers the exam wants. 1 dB = the smallest perceptible change (most people miss it). 3 dB = a "just noticeable" change. 10 dB = perceived as twice as loud (or half as loud). 85 → 95 dB SPL sounds like double.

Lv.3 · the inverse square law

Walk away, lose six 📉

Sound spreads out spherically in every direction. The same energy has to cover a bigger and bigger ball — so the further you stand, the thinner it gets.

★ SKILL GET! Double the distance, drop 6.

The inverse square law: sound energy is inversely proportional to the square of the distance. Double the distance → the sphere's area (4πr²) goes up 4× → energy at any point falls to a quarter → −6 dB. Halve the distance and you gain 6 dB. This is the "6 dB per doubling" rule.

🌍 it's not just sound The inverse square law applies to everything that radiates from a source — light, radio waves, gravity, magnetism, the electric field. Learn it once, use it in half the remaining chapters.
⚠ the honest caveat A true 6 dB per doubling only happens in a near-field (within a few wavelengths of the source) or free-field (no reflections) environment. Put it in a real room with walls and the reflections fill in the gap.
Lv.4 · digital audio

Chapter 3 comes back 💾

Good news: you already know most of this. Anna and Digi had this exact argument last chapter — here it is again, wearing an audio hat.

Same formula as last time, nya — only the name changed. Bit rate is called bandwidth now. Snaps × Shades × Speakers, still. 🐾
Bandwidth = Sample Rate × Bit Depth × Channels bits per second = samples per second × bits per sample × number of channels
sample rate

How often 📸

  • 44.1 kHz — CD, set by the Red Book spec (Sony + Philips, 1980)
  • 48 kHz → captures to 24 kHz
  • 96 kHz → captures to 48 kHz
  • 192 kHz → captures to 96 kHz

44.1 was a trade-off with 1980 optical disc limits vs Nyquist's "twice the highest frequency".

bit depth

How precisely 🖍

  • 16-bit → 65,536 levels → ~96 dB range (CD)
  • 24-bit → ~144 dB range
  • 32-bit → ~192 dB range

Rounding an infinitely variable analog signal to fixed levels creates quantization error.

★ SKILL GET! Six dB per bit.

Three numbers collapse into one rule. 16 × 6 = 96 dB. 24 × 6 = 144 dB. 32 × 6 = 192 dB. Every bit of depth buys you roughly 6 dB more dynamic range — and you never have to memorise the table again.

Lossless audio
50–70% SMALLER · NOTHING LOST
  • FLAC · WavPack · Monkey's Audio · ALAC
  • Needs significant processing power → possible latency
  • Good for high-quality recording and remote streaming
vs
Lossy audio
PSYCHOACOUSTIC · DETAIL BINNED
  • MP3 (= MPEG-2 Audio Layer III) · AAC
  • 320 kbps ≈ 75% smaller · 160 kbps ≈ 90% smaller than CD's 1.4 Mbps
  • Fine for noisy rooms, background music, voice-only paging
📦 uncompressed is the pro default Most professional AV doesn't compress at all — it avoids the processing overhead and latency. Uncompressed formats: PCM (like CD audio), AIFF, WAV, MPEG audio, HDMI audio, and AES3 (also called AES/EBU).

Audio over the network 🌐

protocol

AVB

An IEEE standard. Uncompressed video plus up to 200 channels of 48 kHz/24-bit in real time, using Ethernet frames.

Needs special AVB-enabled switches — and they're not cheap.

protocol

Dante

Runs over TCP/IP. Over gigabit: up to 1,024 channels at 48 kHz/24-bit, or 512 at 96 kHz. Over 100 Mbps: 96 channels.

Fully routable using standard switches and routers. No separate infrastructure.

the standard

AES67

The interoperability standard that simplified the whole mess. Older proprietary options like CobraNet and EtherSound ran audio over Ethernet but not TCP/IP.

★ SKILL GET! AVB needs special switches. Dante doesn't.

That single sentence is the difference the exam cares about. And for streaming: Unicast = one stream each (three clients at 100 kbps really costs you 300 kbps). Multicast = one stream for all, on Class D addresses (224.0.0.0 – 239.255.255.255) — but LAN or private networks only, never the open Internet.

Lv.5 · acoustics

What a wall does to a sound 🧱

Acoustics is more than "hang some fabric on a wall." It covers how sound is produced, and its propagation and control — which is exactly how the exam words it.

★ SKILL GET! Sound hits a wall and becomes a RAT.

Energy is never destroyed — the law of conservation of energy says so. When sound meets a boundary, some combination of exactly three things happens: Reflected · Absorbed · Transmitted. Anna and Digi both chase it.

R · reflected

Bounced back 🪞

Direct / specular / hard — mirror-like, coherent, one direction. Angle of incidence = angle of reflection.

Diffuse — scattered in many directions.

Which one you get depends on how smooth the surface is relative to the wavelength. Energy stays in the room either way.

A · absorbed

Turned into heat 🔥

Porous absorbers — carpet, acoustic tiles, foam, curtains, upholstery, and people. Friction slows the air molecules and the energy becomes low-level heat.

Absorption only works over a limited frequency range — everything outside it still reflects or transmits.

T · transmitted

Escaped next door 🚪

Passes through partitions or travels as structure-borne vibration.

How much gets through depends on the partition's mass, stiffness, air gaps, design and internal structure.

🔁Echo vs reverberation.

➡️

Direct sound takes the straight line, so it always arrives first. Reflected sound takes a longer path, so it always arrives later.

👋

A single late reflection is an echo.

🌊

But when a room has many hard surfaces, reflections stay loud and pile up until they arrive from every direction at once. That dense, persistent mass is reverberation — and it needs a large space. A conference room has bad reflections, not true reverb.

🔇 ambient noise Noise is anything other than the desired signal — and the exam's phrasing is that ambient noise interferes with the message (it doesn't block it, and it certainly doesn't enhance it). Sources: HVAC, equipment fans, office kit, phones, people, traffic, weather, corridors, and structure-borne vibration. Limits should be specified per room type — a gym and a boardroom get very different numbers.
Lv.6 · sound capture

Microphones 🎙

Sound → electricity → sound. Converting energy from one form to another is called transduction, and a device that does it is a transducer.

★ SKILL GET! Transducers at both ends.

The microphone converts acoustic energy → electrical energy. The loudspeaker converts electrical energy → acoustic energy. Both are transducers, sitting at opposite ends of the same electrical path.

The four types you must know

Dynamic
TRIPLE D
  • Diaphragm + coil moving in a magnet — cuts the flux, induces a voltage
  • Durable, economical, handles high SPL
  • Doesn't need power at all
vs
Condenser
TRIPLE C
  • Capacitor — diaphragm + backplate, air as insulator
  • Charge needed → requires power, and has a built-in preamp
  • Crisper — lighter diaphragm, more sensitive, better high frequencies
★ SKILL GET! Dynamic = magnet, no power. Condenser = capacitor, needs power.

Everything else follows. The condenser's preamp is powered by the same supply that charges its plates — and it is not the mic preamp in the mixing console.

type 3

Electret

A type of condenser. Named for the prepolarised material on the diaphragm or backplate, which holds a permanent fixed charge.

That removes the need for high polarising voltage → runs on small batteries or normal phantom power. Physically small, so it's what lavaliers and headmics use.

type 4

MEMS

Built directly onto silicon chips with the same etching used for microprocessors. Variations on condenser or piezo-electric designs.

Tiny = not very sensitive (especially at bass) — but they can be built in arrays, and the amps, DSP and even the A-to-D converter can sit on the same chip, giving a direct digital output.

⚡ phantom power Remote power for condensers: 12 V to 48 V DC, with 48 V the most common. Positive voltage is applied equally to both signal conductors of a balanced circuit, returning through the shield. Because it's equal on both, it doesn't affect the audio and doesn't damage dynamic mics. Usually switchable at the mixer — per channel, per group, or all at once.

Polar patterns — where a mic listens 🎯

🎛 gadget 04 · the pickup patterns cardioid

The mic points up. Filled area = where it hears you.

omnidirectional

Everything, equally

Pickup is uniform in all directions. Good when you want the whole room — an interview, say.

cardioid

The heart ❤️

Pickup primarily from the front. Rejects the sides, with the most rejection at the rear. Named for its heart-shaped plot.

hypercardioid

Tighter

More directional than cardioid — rejects more from the side. Trade-off: some sound is picked up directly at the rear.

supercardioid

Tighter still

Better directionality than hypercardioid, as its side rejection is better. It also has more rear pickup than the hypercardioid.

bidirectional

Figure-eight 8️⃣

Equal pickup in opposite directions, with little or no pickup from the sides.

⚠ heads up — a wording clash Your textbook ranks supercardioid as more directional than hypercardioid. Most of the audio industry says the opposite (hyper is tighter, and super has less rear pickup). Answer the way your book words it — but don't be thrown if a manufacturer's datasheet disagrees. I've drawn the standard shapes above and quoted your book's descriptions verbatim.
spec 1

Sensitivity

How efficiently a mic converts acoustic → electrical. Same SPL in, more volts out = more sensitive. Condensers are more sensitive than dynamics.

Example: −54.5 dBV/Pa. And 1 Pa = 94 dB SPL, the usual reference (sometimes 74 dB SPL = 0.1 Pa).

Low sensitivity ≠ low quality — a singer working close needs a dynamic that won't distort; a presenter a metre back needs a sensitive one.

spec 2

Frequency response

Electrical output across the audible spectrum — the range from lowest to highest the mic can transduce. It's what makes a mic "sound like" itself.

Best on-axis. Move off-axis on a directional mic and you lose level and the frequency response changes. Plotted together, that's a polar plot.

spec 3

Impedance

Impedance = opposition to current in an AC circuit (audio is AC). Resistance = the same but for DC. Both in ohms (Ω).

The one-tenth rule: output impedance should be ≤ 1/10 of the input it feeds. Pro mic ≤ 200 Ω → input ≥ 2 kΩ.

Low-Z ≤ 200 Ω (some to 600 Ω) · High-Z > 25 kΩ. Pro gear is low impedance — less noise, much longer cable runs.

Physical designs 🎤

handheld

Speech or singing. Has internal shock mounting because it's constantly moved.

boundary

Surface-mount, against a table, floor or wall. The surface's reflectivity affects it. Ceiling mounting gives the poorest performance — far from talkers, close to HVAC and projectors.

gooseneck

Flexible stem on lecterns and tables. Shock mounts available to isolate vibration.

shotgun

Named for its shape and long narrow polar pattern. Film and TV — on a fishpole, studio boom, or camera.

instrument

Condenser or dynamic depending on loudness and range. Some use direct mechanical or magnetic pickups.

lavalier & headmic

Worn by the user. Size, appearance and colour are critical — so they're usually electret.

beamforming array

Many condenser or MEMS capsules + DSP to form narrow beams that can be electronically steered. Wall, table or ceiling. Meeting rooms and lecterns.

wireless

Radio mics 📻

RF transmission instead of cable (some systems use IR). Handheld with integrated or plug-on transmitter; lav/headmic into a bodypack; receiver tuned to that frequency.

Selectable frequencies avoid interference, cross-talk and intermodulation. Spread-spectrum frequency hopping minimises cross-talk and stops eavesdropping.

Even the best wireless link is less reliable and less noise-immune than a cable.

cables

Shielded twisted pair

  • A twisted pair of insulated copper conductors — the twisting gives common-mode noise rejection
  • An aluminium foil or braided copper shield around them
  • A protective insulated jacket

Terminated with a 3-pin XLR: socket (female) at the mic end, plug (male) at the processing end. Many circuits at once = a multicore or snake.

★ SKILL GET! X plus L-R. Left and Right, with an X in front.

The exam offers you XRL, RXL and LRX to see if you'll blink. L and R are already the two letters your brain owns in audio — just remember the X goes first. XLR.

📡 spectrum management Running several wireless systems at once — mics, in-ear monitors, comms — requires frequency coordination. A full-spectrum frequency allocation plan is a required part of any install, and must respect local frequency regulations.

Millivolts to many volts 🔌

Four levels, in order, getting bigger. Get these in your head as a staircase and the whole signal chain makes sense.

★ SKILL GET! Millivolts, one volt, many volts.

Mic = a few millivolts, weak and vulnerable to interference — so a microphone preamplifier lifts it to line level before anything else touches it. Line level (~1 V pro, 316 mV consumer) is where all routing and processing happens. Loudspeaker level is the power amp's output: 4 V+ typical, 70/100 V distributed, 100 V+ in huge venues.

🔎 spotting consumer line level Consumer gear gives itself away with 3.5 mm (⅛ in) stereo jacks and RCA (phono) connectors, referenced to 10 kΩ at 1 kHz. Pro line level references 600 Ω at 1 kHz.
compatibility

What not to plug in 💥

Plug a mic straight into a power amp and you'll get almost nothing — mic level is far too weak on its own.

Plug a power amp output into a mic or line input and you will almost certainly damage components.

Powered / active loudspeakers build the whole lot in: mic preamps if they have mic inputs, internal processing at line level, and the power amp.

vocabulary

Gain words 🎚

  • Gain — increasing the level (the amount of amplification)
  • Attenuation — decreasing the level
  • Gain adjustment — any change you make to a level
  • Gain control — the general ability to make those adjustments
  • Unity gain — neither gain nor attenuation; the signal passes through unchanged
⚠ exam trap "A technician changes the level of a signal" → that's a gain adjustment, not attenuation. Attenuation is only the downward half. Unity = unchanged.
Lv.8 · audio devices

The box that does the work 🎛

Mixers, dynamics processors, filters, EQ, delay, DSP and amplifiers — everything between the microphone and the loudspeaker.

mixers

Combine, control, route, process

That's the job, in the book's own four words. Usually more inputs than outputs — an 8×2 mixer is 8 in, 2 out. Also called a mixing console, audio console or mixing desk.

Any mixer with mic inputs has mic preamps. Multiple gain stages between in and out let the operator balance the sources.

automatic mixers

Gate vs gain-share

Gated automatic — opens and closes channels like an on/off switch.

Gain-sharing automatic — turns up channels in use and attenuates the rest, like a volume knob.

Speech only. Music needs a live operator. And don't confuse automatic mixers with automated mixers — the latter are computer-driven with stored presets.

★ SKILL GET! Above the line: Compress and Limit. Below the line: Expand and Gate.

The entire dynamics family in one sentence. Compressors and limiters act on signals above the threshold. Expanders and gates act on signals below it. All four are dynamic processors — they change the dynamic range, the gap between the loudest and quietest parts.

above threshold

Compressor

  • Reduces the level of all signals above an adjustable threshold — keeps loud things from being too loud
  • Amount set by an adjustable ratio
  • Reduces dynamic range
  • Used to prevent distortion
above threshold

Limiter

  • Extreme compression. Prevents high-amplitude signals getting through at all
  • Triggered by peaks and spikes — a dropped microphone — and reacts fast
  • More aggressive ratio than a compressor
  • Used to prevent damage to loudspeakers
below threshold

Expander

  • Properly a downward expander
  • Reduces the level of all signals below the threshold
  • Increases dynamic range
  • Used to reduce unwanted background noise
below threshold

Gate

  • An extreme downward expander
  • Mutes everything below the threshold
  • Signals must exceed the threshold to pass
  • Used to automatically turn off unused microphones
★ SKILL GET! A filter is named for what it lets through.

A low-pass filter passes the lows and attenuates the highs. A high-pass filter passes the highs and attenuates the lows. A notch filter notches out one narrow band. The name is a guest list, not a hit list.

equalizers

Graphic vs parametric

EQs boost (gain) or cut (attenuate) a range of frequencies.

Graphic — the ⅓-octave type with 30 or 31 sliders at fixed frequencies and fixed bandwidths. Named because the sliders form a rough picture of the curve.

Parametric — boost and cut plus adjustable centre frequency and bandwidth (the filter's Q). More flexible.

delay

Waiting for the sound ⏱

Sound travels 343 m/s. The electrical signal travels about 150,000 km/s — roughly half a million times faster.

So under-balcony loudspeakers get the signal essentially instantly while the main speakers' sound is still in the air. Result: an echo. Fix: delay the under-balcony feed so both arrive together.

DSP

One box, many jobs

A microprocessor doing maths on the digital audio stream: mixing, automated level control, filtering, EQ, limiting, compression/expansion, delay, pitch shifting, echo cancellation, feedback suppression, temporal manipulation, matrix routing, loudspeaker processing.

One DSP can replace a rack of gear. Usually programmed from an external computer (serial, USB, Ethernet) or increasingly a web interface.

Everything it does costs time — that's latency, measured in milliseconds, and it must be part of your selection criteria.

power amps

The last box ⚡

The amplifier is the last device before the loudspeakers. It raises line level (~1 V) to loudspeaker level.

Connected with heavier-gauge stranded wire, unshielded, sized by distance and current. The pro connector is the Speakon — rugged, locking, and used exclusively for loudspeaker connections.

Lv.9 · loudspeakers

The end of the line 🔊

Acoustic energy became electrical at the microphone. Here it becomes acoustic again. Most loudspeakers are simply acoustic drivers mounted in an enclosure.

★ SKILL GET! The crossover is the traffic cop, not the car.

No single driver can cover the whole spectrum accurately or efficiently, so cabinets use several. A crossover is an electrical frequency-dividing device / network circuit that sends each driver only the frequencies it handles well. The exam offers you three answers that describe the loudspeaker instead — don't take the bait.

drivers

Who plays what 🎚

  • Tweeters — high frequencies
  • Horns — mid to high
  • Cone / midrange — midrange
  • Woofers — low
  • Subwoofers — lower still

The crossover can sit before amplification, or on the amp's input to a multidriver cabinet. Low-power cabinets often use an unpowered (passive) crossover network inside.

sensitivity

Efficiency, not accuracy

How well it converts electrical → acoustic. Written like 88 dB / 1 W @ 1 m — meaning 88 dB SPL measured 1 metre away with 1 watt in.

As with mics, sensitivity is not a measure of accuracy. Speakers are picked for a job.

coverage

Response & polar patterns

Best on-axis; off-axis loses level and changes frequency response — same as directional mics.

A quoted dispersion pattern only holds over a limited high-to-mid range. Low frequencies spread out fast because of their long wavelengths, so controlling them needs very large waveguides and enclosures. Plotted together = a polar plot.

impedance

4, 8 or 16 Ω

A nominal rating — impedance is frequency-dependent, so it isn't true across the whole range.

Know the total impedance of the load on your amplifier to: achieve optimum volume, avoid wasting power, avoid overloading and damaging the amplifier, prevent loudspeaker damage, reduce distortion and noise, and avoid uneven distribution.

🎯 three jobs a loudspeaker does Communication (PA, intercom, evacuation, radio) · Sound reinforcement (lectures, presentations, concerts, theatre, artist foldback) · Sound reproduction (playback of recorded material and soundtracks). Built for portability or permanent install.
Lv.10 · monitoring, circuits & feedback

Keeping it clean 📈

You know the chain and the levels. Now: how to check them, how to keep noise out, and how to stop the squeal.

★ SKILL GET! Analog has headroom. Digital has a brick wall.

With analog metering, aim for around 0 dBu (775 mV) — and it's fine to pop above it now and then. With digital metering you must never exceed 0 dBFS (Full Scale). Anything past it is clipped and distorted, permanently.

🔍Setting levels, in order.

1️⃣

Before any power amp goes on, check you're getting clean signals on every mixer channel you'll use. Set the mic preamp and every other gain stage, verifying nothing distorts — meters plus headphones.

2️⃣

Then: amp inputs all the way down → amp on → slowly bring the inputs up until you reach the SPL you want.

3️⃣

Verify nothing is distorted, by listening and monitoring. Too low gives you poor signal-to-noise; too high gives you distortion.

💡

Golden rule: never leave any single gain stage at maximum — it drives up noise and distortion through the whole path.

★ SKILL GET! Two wires that disagree cancel the noise.

In a balanced circuit the signal travels on two identical parallel wires. At the receiver they're combined: the wanted signal adds up stronger, and any interference picked up by both wires cancels out. That cancellation is common mode rejection. It costs more, and pro audio uses it wherever possible.

Balanced
TWO SIGNAL WIRES + SHIELD
  • Common mode rejection cancels interference
  • Shielded twisted pair, conductors twisted around each other
  • Connector: the 3-pin XLR
  • Long cable runs are fine
vs
Unbalanced
ONE SIGNAL WIRE · "SINGLE-ENDED"
  • No inverted twin → every picked-up noise reaches the output
  • The shield doubles as the return path
  • Connectors: RCA (phono) and 3.5 mm jack
  • Severely limited in distance
⚠ the TRS trap A three-section 3.5 mm TRS jack looks like it might be balanced — it isn't. It carries two separate unbalanced signals (left and right) plus a common return. Count the pins: unbalanced gear has one pin per signal plus a return; balanced uses the 3-pin XLR.

📢Feedback — the squeal.

🔁

Acoustic feedback is the howl you get when a system's output is fed back into its input — the loudspeakers are picked up by the microphones and re-amplified, over and over. A system doing this is called unstable.

⚠️

Two causes only: a mic too close to the front of a loudspeaker, or gain turned up too high somewhere.

best practice

Five ways to kill feedback

  • Keep each microphone as close to its source as possible
  • Keep loudspeakers in front of, and as far from, the mics as practical
  • Use directional microphones with suitable polar patterns
  • Use loudspeakers whose coverage hits only the audience area
  • Keep all gains at the minimum needed
Lv.11 · audio system applications

Six systems in the wild 🏟

The whole chain, assembled into the six systems the chapter names.

1 · sound reinforcement / PA

Make it louder 📣

If it can't be heard unamplified, you add mics, mixers, processors, amps and speakers. Two flavours: music reinforcement needs full bandwidth and high SPL; speech reinforcement can get away with less, because the human voice has limited bandwidth.

In live production it's often just called a PA (public address) system.

2 · mix-minus

Everything except 🚫

A reinforcement system where some elements are deliberately left out. Used where feedback is likely because mics and loudspeakers are close together.

You build a separate subsystem per loudspeaker zone, and each one mixes the mics minus the ones that would cause local echo or feedback. Also used for musicians' local foldback.

3 · intercom & paging

Two-way vs one-way 📞

Intercom = two-way, like a telephone. A central unit with local stations and possibly satellite/remote stations.

Paging = one-way only. From supermarket announcements up to emergency notification — where the emphasis is on coverage and intelligibility.

4 · playback

No microphones 💿

Like music reinforcement but with no mics at all — it just plays prerecorded material and data streams. One well-designed system often covers speech reinforcement, music reinforcement and playback.

5 · conferencing

Talking to elsewhere 🌍

Multi-mic modules on tables or suspended from the ceiling; big groups get individual mics as part of a larger AV system. Typically includes DSP-based acoustic echo cancellation, gating and level compression.

Audio for videoconferencing is the same thing, with the mics placed more discreetly.

6 · sound masking

The backwards one 🤫

Every other system tries to minimise background noise. Sound masking deliberately adds it — to reduce the intelligibility of distant conversations and give speech privacy. It also covers up distractions from mechanical systems and street noise.

🤫 remember the odd one out If a question asks which system wants noise, it is always sound masking. It is the only one in the entire chapter that adds noise on purpose.

The cheat sheet 📋 ✧

Too big for one list, so it's grouped. Four blocks, sixteen lines.

🌊 sound itself

  1. Propagation is how sound moves through air. It's compression waves needing a compressible medium — no medium, no sound.
  2. Wavelength is a ruler, frequency is a clock. Wavelength = distance between two points exactly one cycle apart. λ = 343/f, and 343 = 7³.
  3. Low and long, high and short. 20 Hz = 17.2 m · 20 kHz = 17.2 mm. 10 octave bands, each a doubling.
  4. The fundamental IS the first harmonic. Harmonics are whole-number multiples; their proportions give timbre.

📊 decibels & distance

  1. Power gets Ten, Volts and Distance get Twenty. 10 log(P1/P2) · 20 log(V1/V2). A dB is a ratio, not an absolute.
  2. 0 dB SPL = the threshold of hearing (20 µPa). 0 dBu = 775 mV · 0 dBV = 1 V · 0 dBm = 1 mW.
  3. One barely, three clearly, ten doubly. 1 dB smallest perceptible · 3 dB just noticeable · 10 dB twice as loud.
  4. Double the distance, drop 6. Inverse square law — true only in near-field or free-field.

💾 digital & the room

  1. Bandwidth = Sample Rate × Bit Depth × Channels — Snaps × Shades × Speakers, again.
  2. Six dB per bit. 16-bit→96 dB · 24-bit→144 dB · 32-bit→192 dB. CD = 44.1 kHz/16-bit, Red Book, 1980.
  3. AVB needs special switches, Dante doesn't. Unicast = one stream each · Multicast = one for all, Class D, LAN only.
  4. Sound hits a wall and becomes a RAT — Reflected, Absorbed, Transmitted. One late reflection = echo; a dense persistent crowd of them = reverberation.

🔌 the chain

  1. Dynamic = magnet, no power. Condenser = capacitor, needs power (12–48 V phantom, 48 V typical). Electret = condenser with a permanent charge. Cardioid rejects most at the rear.
  2. Millivolts, one volt, many volts. Mic mV → line ~1 V (consumer 316 mV) → loudspeaker 4 V+. Changing a level = gain adjustment; unity = unchanged. Cable = shielded twisted pair + XLR. One-tenth rule on impedance.
  3. Above the line: Compress and Limit. Below the line: Expand and Gate. A filter is named for what it lets through. The amp is the last box before the loudspeakers.
  4. The crossover is the traffic cop, not the car — an electrical frequency-dividing circuit. Balanced = two wires + common mode rejection. Feedback = output re-entering the input.
