Lv.1 · types of networks

Every network is an ___AN 🏠

A network is just a group of devices connected so they can talk. They're categorised by the area they cover — their scale. Which means the whole list is one letter plus "area network".

★ SKILL GET! Living room, Campus, City, World.

The size ladder is just L → C → M → W. LAN is a building. CAN is a campus or cluster of buildings. MAN is a suburb or city. WAN is a state or country — and the Internet is the largest WAN, covering the earth. LANs join WANs through routers.

the size ladder

L · C · M · W

  • LAN — a confined area: a building or living complex. Short distance, and generally owned or controlled by the end user
  • CAN — multiple LANs in a limited area, like a university campus
  • MAN — a suburb or a city
  • WAN — a state or country. The Internet is the biggest one

LANs and WLANs are the most common, and they're the building blocks CANs, MANs and WANs are made from.

the odd ones out

W · S · V · P

  • WLAN — a LAN, but wireless
  • SAN — Storage: a high-speed special-purpose network interconnecting data-storage devices
  • VLAN — Virtual: devices on separate LAN segments joined into a logical group, spanning the LANs they're connected to
  • PAN — Personal: limited-range wireless for one person or a small workgroup
🐈 the smallest network A network can be as small as two connected computers or as large as the Internet. Anna and Digi arguing across a table is, technically, a PAN.
Lv.2 · network topology

The shape of the wires 🕸

Devices on a network are nodes. Physical topology is the shape the network makes when it's connected. Logical topology is the way information flows through it.

★ SKILL GET! Star has a middle. Bus has two ends. Ring has none. Mesh has everything.

Four shapes, told apart by counting their ends. Star — every node to one central point, a switch or router; the most common topology in general use. Bus — one cable looping node to node along a linear path. Ring — the same, but the two ends are joined into a continuous loop. Mesh — every node connected to at least one other, with many possible paths.

star

One point in the middle ⭐

All nodes connect to a central switch or router, which have multiple connection capabilities. The most common topology in general use.

bus

A single line 🚌

All nodes on one cable, looping from node to node. Used for control systems like lighting. Each node has a unique number set in software or by a mechanical switch.

ring

A line with its ends joined 💍

Like a bus, but the ends join into a continuous loop. A break doesn't stop the flow, because redundant paths can be created. Telecoms and WAN Internet services use a mesh ring for redundancy.

mesh

Everyone to everyone 🕸

Each node connects via bridges, switches or routers to at least one other. Data takes many possible paths → high resilience to failure.

Used for ad hoc wireless where nodes appear and vanish unpredictably and one node relays for another, plus building automation and the Internet of Things.

⚠ exam trap #1 "Connects devices in sequence along a linear path" is the bus. Ring is also a sequence — but a ring has no ends, so it isn't linear. Count the ends: two = bus, zero = ring.
Lv.3 · ethernet

The house rules of the LAN 🔌

Ethernet is the most commonly used method of transferring data on a LAN — an IEEE standard governing how computers exchange information over the medium.

★ SKILL GET! Ethernet tries its best and apologises later.

Ethernet is packet-based: big files get broken into chunks of 1,500 bits or smaller. Packets can take different paths and arrive out of order, so the receiving device must track what's missing and either ask again or shrug. That's why Ethernet is called a "best-effort" delivery system.

the standard

IEEE 802

Specifies the physical transmission media (cable, fibre, wireless) and how signal information is handled (frames or packets).

Revised periodically as 802.x.x, where the x's are revision numbers.

the connector

8P8C, a.k.a. RJ-45

Eight-position, eight-conductor modular connector, terminated onto the cable. Inexpensive and easy to install in the field.

Two wiring formats: T568-A and T568-B — and the overwhelming majority of installations and premade cables use T568-B.

★ SKILL GET! A on one end, B on the other — that's a crossover.

Terminate T568-A at one end and T568-B at the other and you have a crossover cable. It lets two devices connect directly — two computers, say — without a switch or router, which would normally do the crossover electronically.

Lv.4 · fibre & wireless

Light down a hair, or no wire at all 💡

Fibre carries information coded in a beam of light through a glass or plastic pipe — a strand as thin as human hair. Used where runs are long and speeds are high.

★ SKILL GET! Yellow goes far. Orange stays near.

Single-mode — light travels a single path, identified by a yellow outer jacket, and reaches farther because of how the cable is built. Multimode — multiple light paths, identified by an orange jacket. Yellow = one = far.

speed

Absurd headroom

Fibre easily handles LAN speeds of 40 Gbps, and goes higher just by upgrading the transmitter and receiver.

It's been tested to hundreds of terabits per second (1 Tbps = 1,000 Gbps) — which makes it a good long-term investment in cable plant.

connectors

Twist, pull, click

  • ST — insert and twist, much like a BNC connector
  • LC — the "push, pull" connector
  • SC — inserts and clicks into place

S-T = Stick and Twist. S-C = Stick and Click. The letters do the work for two of the three.

📶Wi-Fi, and what actually limits it.

📡

Wi-Fi is defined by IEEE 802.11, continually revised to keep up with demand — developers are working toward a theoretical 20 Gbps. The device handling the connection is an access point.

📉

As signal strength weakens, the connection slows down. It doesn't stop and it doesn't stay constant — it degrades. Speed depends on RF signal strength, the 802.11 revision, and how many users are on it.

🧱

Some systems claim 70 m (about 230 ft), but the real limit is the antennas at both ends, the obstructions between them, and the transmit power — not the number on the box.

★ SKILL GET! 802.11 for the air. 802.1x for the door.

Two numbers that look almost identical and mean completely different things. 802.11 = Wi-Fi. 802.1x = port-based network access control, the authentication you'll meet in Level 11. The exam puts them in the same list of options on purpose.

Lv.5 · the OSI reference model

Seven layers of cake 🎂

Built by the ISO — yes, they know the initials don't match — to standardise communication between devices worldwide. Seven layers, each with a specific duty, processed in a specific sequential order.

★ SKILL GET! Please Don't Nibble The Sugar, Precious Anna.

Bottom to top: Physical · Data Link · Network · Transport · Session · Presentation · Application. Going the other way, the classic is All People Seem To Need Data Processing. Learn one direction properly and you can always count to the other.

🎛 gadget 02 · tap a layer Layer 1
★ SKILL GET! Segments, Packets, Frames — four, three, two.

Data changes its name as it falls down the stack. Big streams from the upper layers are broken into segments at the Transport layer (4). At the Network layer (3) destination and control information is added, making a packet. At the Data Link layer (2) packets are encapsulated into frames carrying the physical destination, such as the MAC address. The Physical layer (1) puts it on the wire.

🎛 gadget 03 · watch a file fall down the stack layer 7
Application

Each layer wraps the last one in its own header. That's encapsulation.

🎯 what AV people are asked about The book flags layers 7 and 1 as the ones AV professionals should watch. And the exam's favourite: cabling and patchbays are Layer 1, the Physical layer.
Lv.6 · the TCP/IP networking model

The same cake, four slices 📦

The Internet Engineering Task Force specifies the standards for TCP/IP — SMTP for e-mail, FTP for file transfer, DNS for domain names, HTTP for the World Wide Web, and the IP address numbering systems themselves.

★ SKILL GET! Squash the top three, squash the bottom two.

TCP/IP nominates only four layers, and they map onto OSI exactly: Application = OSI 7 + 6 + 5 · Transport = OSI 4 · Internet = OSI 3 (the Network layer) · Network Access = OSI 2 + 1. The middle two match one-for-one; only the ends collapse.

Lv.7 · network interface cards & MAC addresses

The number you were born with 🆔

To join a network a device needs an interface — a network interface card (NIC). Once separate cards, now often built into the device's main circuitry. Wireless connections count as NICs too, since they interface RF to traditional wired cabling.

★ SKILL GET! MAC is your birth certificate. IP is your mailing address.

A MAC address is the actual hardware address of a NIC — globally unique, part of the IEEE 802 standard, and burned in at the factory. An IP address is where you happen to live right now, and it can change. That one sentence answers two exam questions.

anatomy

48 bits, six pairs

A 48-bit number written as six groups of two hexadecimal digits, separated by hyphens or colons:

01-23-45-67-89-ab
01:23:45:67:89:ab

The first part identifies the manufacturer, the second is a serial number for the product or component — drawn from a table assigned to makers of Ethernet-capable devices.

why it matters

One per network 🔒

Because of how MAC addresses work, there can be only one device with that number on any given network.

It's one of the lowest levels of communication on the network — one of the first pieces of the structure. Switches and routers store MAC address locations so they can send traffic efficiently to a specific device.

Lv.8 · internet protocol addressing

Where the device actually lives 📮

The device has a way to connect (the NIC) and a unique number (the MAC). The IP address defines the exact device and its location on a network. IP lives on layer 3.

★ SKILL GET! Name is what. Address is where. Route is how. IP does where.

Straight from the book's own distinction: a name indicates what you seek, an address indicates where it is, a route indicates how to get there — and IP deals primarily with addresses. All four words appear as options on the exam.

IPv4
SINCE 1982 · FOUR OCTETS
  • Four 8-bit chunks called octets — 192.168.1.25
  • Really a 32-digit binary number:
    11000000.10101000.00000001.00011001
  • 2³² = 4,294,967,296 possible addresses
  • Left part = network prefix, right part = network host
vs
IPv6
EIGHT GROUPS OF FOUR HEX
  • Eight groups of four hexadecimal digits
    FEDC:BA98:7654:3210:FEDC:BA98:7654:3210
  • 2¹²⁸ ≈ 340 undecillion addresses
  • Letters A–F stand for the digits 10–15
  • Can use the MAC address as part of its numbering to guarantee uniqueness — though that isn't recommended in all cases
  • No interoperability with IPv4 — it's a parallel, independent network
★ SKILL GET! The mask hides the network and leaves the host.

An IP address has two components — network address and host address — and the subnet mask separates them. It masks the network part and leaves the host number to identify the device. Without a subnet mask a device won't be recognised properly on a network. Subnetting subdivides IP networks logically, increasing performance and enhancing security.

Static
ASSIGNED BY HAND · STAYS PUT

For devices that must always be findable — a videoconferencing system, IP-controlled equipment. Sometimes you have no choice.

  • IP address — required
  • Subnet mask — required
  • Device name
  • DNS server
  • Gateway
vs
Dynamic
DHCP · TEMPORARY

Tick "obtain IP address automatically" and a DHCP server reads the device's MAC address and assigns it an IP.

  • Held only for a lease time, then usually auto-renewed if still connected
  • Good: easy to manage, no two devices get the same address, more people can connect
  • Bad: you never know what your address will be next time
  • Hybrid: reserve a block, and enter each static device's MAC in a table so DHCP hands it the same address every time
⚠ exam trap #2 255.255.255.0 is the common "Class C" mask and allows 254 devices. 255.0.0.0 handles more than 16 million. And when the exam asks what a subnet mask indicates the number of — the word it wants is devices, not computers.
Lv.9 · the domain name system

The café's phone book 📖

DNS is a hierarchical, distributed database that maps names to data such as IP addresses. A DNS server keeps track of the devices on the network and matches their names so they can be found or wired into control and monitoring systems.

It's much easier to remember ietf.org than 4.31.198.44. That's the entire pitch, and it's a good one 📖
★ SKILL GET! DHCP hands out the numbers. DNS remembers the names.

When they work together you may never need to know a device's IP address at all — only its name. That makes a network far simpler to manage, because the address doesn't need to be static, and the entire addressing scheme could change without affecting communication.

Lv.10 · switches, routers, gateways & bridges

Four boxes, four jobs 🔀

The hardware building blocks. They look similar in a rack and they are not the same thing at all.

★ SKILL GET! Switch knows MACs. Router knows IPs. Gateway knows the way out. Bridge knows two languages.

The whole section in one line. A switch stores MAC addresses in memory and looks up destinations. A router reads the destination IP address and works at the layers above a switch — Network and Transport. A gateway is the topmost router in the hierarchy, the one that connects to an outside network. A bridge connects two different types of network by translating one protocol into another.

switch

The MAC librarian 🔀

As each device connects, the switch collects its MAC address and stores it in memory. When one device wants another, the switch looks up the destination in memory and sends it there.

Switches also let connected devices run at their own maximum speeds — put a 100 Mbps and a 1 Gbps device on a 1 Gbps switch and the fast one doesn't slow down.

switch types

Unmanaged vs managed

Unmanaged — you plug it in and connect devices. That's it. No adjustments. It just works.

Managed — the IT manager can adjust port speeds, VLANs, QoS settings, traffic monitoring and more. What you'll find in most complex or multifunction networks.

router & gateway

Up the hierarchy 🧭

Routers work together in a hierarchy — one router can control the behaviour of routers beneath it. They also allow for redundancy.

The gateway is the top-level router. All traffic must eventually travel through it to get outside the local network. It passes traffic to the routers below, which look to it to find DNS names not on the local network. Some devices won't operate without a gateway address.

bridge

The translator 🌉

Connects two different types of network by translating one protocol to another.

The everyday example: a broadband modem, which bridges the Ethernet protocol to a WAN protocol.

⚠ exam trap #3 — the nastiest in the chapter "A _____ sends packets to different locations on a network and connects to outside networks." Both Router and Gateway are offered, and the first half describes a router perfectly. The deciding phrase is "connects to outside networks" — that's the gateway, the topmost router. If both are on the list, look for the words outside or external.
Lv.11 · network security

The bouncer with a list 🔥

The purpose of network security is to prevent unauthorized users from accessing a LAN. It's implemented at many points, but most defences operate at the boundaries — and the first boundary is the physical network interface.

Think of the wall port as an extension of the switch to the wall. You can configure it so only certain MAC addresses get in from that point. 🔒
★ SKILL GET! A firewall is a bouncer with a list.

Firewalls are among the most common network security technologies — software or dedicated hardware. Their primary job: control incoming and outgoing network traffic by analysing packets and deciding whether they should be allowed through, based on a set of rules. That sentence is the exam answer, almost word for word.

technique 1

Packet filtering

Rules decide whether a packet passes. Configured by the network administrator and applied based on the protocol header of each packet.

technique 2

NAT

Network address translation alters IP address information in packet headers as the packet crosses a routing device.

It lets devices with private, unregistered addresses reach the Internet through one registered address, conserving address space, and it hides the original source — from outside, everything appears to come from the NAT server.

technique 3

Port forwarding

Combines NAT and packet filtering. The firewall inspects the packet against the filtering rules and is also configured to translate certain external ports to private addresses inside the network.

★ SKILL GET! The address of the building, but not the apartment number.

The book's own line for why NAT protects you. Data that arrives at the NAT server without being requested by a client has nowhere to go — it's blocked, and an intruder can't trace the path beyond the edge of the network.

NAC

Network access control

A group of technologies chosen to align with an organisation's security policy. Any device trying to connect must comply with that policy — how it's configured, what software it runs. Once connected, NAC controls what it can reach, based on user actions and identities.

⚠ Some AV devices have trouble with NAC because they aren't fully functional computers.

ACL

Access control list

When a network is broken into subnets or broadcast domains, routers may provide additional security through an ACL, which controls what travels through a router based on the type of data traffic, source, and/or destination.

802.1x

The airport gate ✈️

Port-based NAC. An IEEE standard requiring authentication before a device may connect through a certain port — the user must be verified by an authentication server first.

The book's own analogy: like a security agent checking your ID and ticket before letting you into the terminal.

🤝 what this means for your actual job AV professionals designing or installing networked systems usually won't be responsible for network security. The job is to coordinate with the client's IT department so the right AV traffic can cross firewalls — and to list the ports and protocols needed as specifically and narrowly as possible, which you get from the device manufacturers' specifications. AVIXA publishes Recommended Practices for Security in Networked AV Systems.

The cheat sheet 📋 ✧

Four blocks, sixteen lines.

🏠 shapes & wires

  1. Living room, Campus, City, World — L/C/M/W AN. The Internet is the largest WAN. Plus Wireless, Storage, Virtual (logical group), Personal.
  2. Star has a middle, Bus has two ends, Ring has none, Mesh has everything. Star is the most common; bus is the linear path; mesh gives resilience and runs IoT.
  3. Ethernet is IEEE 802, packet-based at 1,500 bits or smaller, and "best effort". Connector = 8P8C / RJ-45; T568-B is almost everything.
  4. A on one end, B on the other = crossover, which links two devices with no switch.
  5. Yellow goes far, orange stays near — single-mode (one path) vs multimode. ST = stick and twist, SC = stick and click, LC = push-pull.
  6. 802.11 for the air, 802.1x for the door. Weak Wi-Fi signal → the connection slows down. The box handling it is an access point.

🎂 the models

  1. Please Don't Nibble The Sugar, Precious Anna — Physical, Data Link, Network, Transport, Session, Presentation, Application.
  2. Cabling and patchbays are Layer 1. Switches and bridges = 2 · routers = 3 · gateways = 4.
  3. Segments, Packets, Frames — four, three, two. Named as data falls down the stack.
  4. TCP/IP squashes the top three and the bottom two: Application (7-6-5) · Transport (4) · Internet (3) · Network Access (2-1). Standards come from the IETF.

🆔 names & numbers

  1. MAC is your birth certificate, IP is your mailing address. MAC = 48-bit, six pairs of hex, globally unique, maker + serial. The interface itself is a NIC.
  2. Name is what, address is where, route is how — IP does where. IPv4 = four octets, 32-bit. IPv6 = eight groups of four hex, 2¹²⁸.
  3. The mask hides the network and leaves the host. 255.255.255.0 → 254 devices; 255.0.0.0 → 16 million+. Manual setup requires IP address + subnet mask.
  4. DHCP hands out the numbers (reads your MAC, gives a lease); DNS remembers the names.

🔀 boxes & doors

  1. Switch knows MACs. Router knows IPs. Gateway knows the way out. Bridge knows two languages. Unmanaged = plug it in, no adjustments; managed = ports, VLANs, QoS. ⚠ If the question says "connects to outside networks", it's the gateway, not the router.
  2. A firewall is a bouncer with a list — controls traffic in and out by rules. Techniques: packet filtering, NAT ("the address of the building, but not the apartment number"), and port forwarding (both together). 802.1x = the airport gate; ACL lives in the router.
