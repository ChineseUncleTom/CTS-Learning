Lv.1 · the nature of light

Waves, or tiny bullets 💡

Two theories: light is bursts of electromagnetic waves, or light is particles called photons. Both are right, which is annoying — sometimes it behaves as one, sometimes the other. For AV work, think of it as waves.

★ SKILL GET! Wavelength is the filing system.

Every type of electromagnetic radiation is categorised by its wavelength — that's the answer to question 1, and the trap options are "spectrum" and "visibility". Gamma rays sit at about 10⁻¹² m; TV and radio at 10⁵ m. Visible light is a thin sliver in between.

🌈The sliver we can actually see.

📐

Visible light runs roughly 380 nm to 750 nm, where a nanometre is 10⁻⁹ metres. Everything you have ever seen lives in that band.

🔴

Red is long, violet is short. Red 620–750 · green 495–570 · blue 450–495 · violet 380–450. Same rule as sound last chapter — long wavelength, low frequency.

Lv.2 · units of light measure

Four units, one grid 📏

This is the messiest table in the chapter, and it becomes easy the moment you stop memorising four things and start memorising two questions: is the light landing or leaving, and are we in the US or everywhere else?

★ SKILL GET! Incident lands. Reflected leaves. Feet are American.

Incident meters measure light coming directly from a source — a lamp, projector, monitor. Reflected (spot) meters measure light bouncing off a screen, wall or face. Then: anything with "foot" in the name is US customary; the rest is SI.

fundamental 1

Candela 🕯

The base unit of luminous intensity. Latin for candle — it happens to match the output of a historic standard wax candle.

fundamental 2

Lumen 💡

The unit of luminous flux — the photons from a 1-candela source passing through 1 m² on a sphere of 1 m radius.

The usual unit for projector output. Different bodies measure it differently; the most quoted worldwide is the ANSI method, despite no longer being the international standard.

⚠ exam wording Question 2 asks what's "generally taken at a task area like a video screen" and wants lux. Strictly, foot-candle measures the same thing — both are illuminance — so the question is really testing that you know lux is the standard international unit. Read those options carefully.
Lv.3 · the inverse square law, again

You already know this one 📉

Same law as the sound chapter, new victim. Light radiates in all directions, so the same energy covers a bigger and bigger sphere.

Sound lost 6 dB per doubling. Light loses three quarters per doubling. Same law, different clothes, nya 🐾
★ SKILL GET! A quarter left means three quarters lost.

Double the distance and intensity falls to 1/2² = a quarter of what it was. The exam asks it the other way round: illumination decreases by 75 percent. Same fact, inverted — and "50 percent" is sitting right there to catch you.

Lv.4 · colour, temperature & CRI

Three dimensions of a colour 🎨

Every possible colour is pinned down by three numbers — that's the Munsell system, drawn as a cylinder.

dimension 1

Hue

The underlying spectrum colour — red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet.

dimension 2

Value / brightness

The greyscale value — where it sits between white and black.

dimension 3

Saturation / chroma

How much of the hue — from pale pastel tint to strong bold colour.

★ SKILL GET! Light adds RGB. Paint subtracts RYB.

Your retina has three cone types — red, green, blue, so those are the primary colours of light, mixed additively. Printing inks are cyan (B+G), magenta (R+B), yellow (R+G). But pigments that absorb light mix subtractively, and there the primaries are red, yellow and blue.

🌙 everyone goes colour-blind at night Cone cells stop working at very low light, so in near-darkness everybody has monochrome (scotopic) vision. That's the excuse behind those slightly clumsy blue-tinted "day-for-night" scenes in film.

Colour temperature 🔥

★ SKILL GET! Hot is blue, cool is orange — the opposite of the tap.

Colour temperature is measured in kelvin — never "degrees". And it runs backwards from every bathroom tap you've ever used: higher K = bluer light, lower K = more orange light. Cameras are far more sensitive to it than your eye, which is why white balance is critical.

this is "white"
3200 K

Candle flame ≈ 1900 K · tungsten ≈ 3200 K · daylight ≈ 5600 K · blue sky ≫ 9000 K

🎯Colour Rendering Index.

💯

CRI says how accurately a light shows the colours of what it lights. 100 is perfect — and only sources that make light by incandescence get it: a tungsten filament, a candle flame, the sun's photosphere.

📉

Everything else scores lower — sodium vapour, fluorescent phosphors, LEDs. High-CRI versions exist but cost more and are less efficient.

📷

Cameras struggle below CRI 60, even after a full white balance. Matters enormously when a client's brand colour has to be right.

★ SKILL GET! Ambient light is the enemy of contrast.

Ambient light is any light in the room other than the displayed image. It hits the screen and walls, bounces into the viewing area, and competes with the picture by reducing contrast and washing it out. Question 4 wants exactly one word: it negatively affects image quality. Fix it by extinguishing or masking unnecessary sources and tightly controlling the necessary ones.

Lv.5 · image capture

How a camera thinks 📷

Video is just a series of consecutive still images, like frames on a strip of film — except a grid of microscopic sensors replaces the chemistry.

focal length

Short sees wide 👀

The distance between the lens and the image sensor when the subject is in focus, in mm. It sets the angle of view.

Wide-angle = short focal length = sees more. Telephoto = long = sees less. A zoom lens varies it, and gets quoted at both extremes.

back focus

Staying sharp while zooming

The adjustment that keeps a zoom lens in focus across its whole range. Two steps, repeated:

  • 1. Zoom fully in on a back-focus chart, adjust until sharp
  • 2. Zoom fully out to widest, adjust again
  • Repeat until focus holds from wide to telephoto
★ SKILL GET! Big number, small hole, deep field.

The aperture is the opening; the iris controls it; it's measured in f-stops. The larger the f-stop number, the smaller the opening and the less light. f/2 lets in more than f/2.8 but less than f/1.4, and each step is half or double the light. Then: the smaller the aperture, the greater the depth of field — so a big f-number gives you a deep zone of focus. A pinhole has enormous depth of field and needs no focusing at all.

Each stop halves or doubles the light. Watch the focus band grow as the hole shrinks.

🌒 the dim-room catch Dimly lit scenes have a smaller depth of field — because you must open the iris wide to get enough light in, and a wide aperture gives a shallow field. Low light and deep focus are enemies.
imager & prism

One chip or three

Three-chip cameras use a prism plus precise dichroic filters to split the beam into red, green and blue, one imager each. Broadcast and digital cinema.

Single-chip cameras have no prism — colour filters are applied to the imager pixel by pixel during manufacture. Most cameras are single-imager.

imagers

CMOS & CCD

Arrays of hundreds of thousands of light-sensing elements. A pixel (picture element) is the group of red, green and blue elements making one colour point — though the individual sensors get called pixels too.

Frame rates: obsolete NTSC 29.94 fps, obsolete PAL 25 fps; digital and large-format run 24 to 120 fps. Higher rate = bigger signal + better motion accuracy.

shutter

Stopping the overload

Too much light overloads an imager. The electronic shutter regulates how long the imager accepts light; shortening it lowers the output voltage and prevents overload.

Shutter speed affects only the imagers — not the video output frame rate. Fast motion needs a higher shutter speed, or the picture looks jerky and stuttered.

Lv.6 · video signals

Three colours and two edges 📶

A video signal has exactly two groups of components: colour signals and synchronisation signals.

★ SKILL GET! R, G, B, H, V — three colours and two edges.

The five basic elements of a video signal, containing everything needed for a complete image. A full-bandwidth RGBHV signal therefore needs five cables, one each. H sync times the left/right edges; V sync times top and bottom, and begins at the bottom-right pixel. Without them the picture rolls sideways and vertically.

★ SKILL GET! Switch in the gap, or you crash.

Sync also lets you change sources cleanly. Switching during the short interval between complete frames is vertical interval switching — the preferred method for all video systems. Switching at any other moment is crash switching, and it produces a burst of instability until the system re-synchronises.

📺Scan rates, i and p.

↔️

Horizontal scan rate = lines displayed per second, in kHz. Vertical scan rate = complete fields per second, in Hz. Many horizontal scans fit inside one vertical scan, so horizontal always has the higher frequency.

🪜

Old displays couldn't draw every line at once, so they drew odd lines, then even lines — that's interlaced, the i in 480i. Its rate is half the vertical scan rate. All lines in one pass is progressive — the p in 1080p.

🎚

Bandwidth is the range of frequencies a circuit can pass. In video: vertical sync is the slow end (Hz), horizontal sync is the middle (kHz), and fine picture detail is the fast end (MHz).

🎨 video bit depth Colour bit depth has mostly stayed at 8 bits per channel (RGB). UHD Blu-ray brought 10 bits per channel with wide-colour-gamut tech, and some composition software works at 16 bits per channel — but displays that can show it aren't widely available yet. HDR is increasingly popular for capturing subtle luminance detail.
🎛 bandwidth limiting Needed to squeeze complex signals into a limited band, achieved through filtering and encoding — RGB values get reduced to smaller values. It takes less bandwidth, but encoding and decoding add noise and artifacts.
Lv.7 · digital signals & connections

What's on the end of the cable 🔌

Digital video devices also output RGBHV — usually with control signals and often audio riding along too.

the original

DVI

Digital Visual Interface — the first accepted digital standard for computers. Versions: DVI, DVI-D, DVI-I, Mini-DVI.

the everywhere one

HDMI

Builds on DVI by adding audio, EDID device control, HDCP content protection and Ethernet. Backward-compatible with DVI. Comes as HDMI, Mini and Micro.

the rival

DisplayPort

Also builds on DVI's success, carrying video and audio in a similar yet not completely compatible format. Also available as Mini-DisplayPort.

one cable to rule them

HDBaseT

Carries HDMI + Ethernet + control + USB + 100 W of DC power down a single standard UTP (Cat 5e and beyond) cable, terminated in a normal 8P8C / RJ45 connector.

broadcast

SDI

Serial Digital Interface, from SMPTE, over BNC-terminated 75 Ω coax — the cable pro analog video already used. HD-SDI, 6G-SDI (4K), 24G-SDI (8K). Carries audio and control, but no content protection. Also runs over fibre.

general purpose

USB & Ethernet

Since USB 3.1, the type-C connector can switch into Alt mode to carry HDMI or DisplayPort. Ethernet carries many streaming video formats over wired, fibre and wireless.

★ SKILL GET! HDMI is DVI plus sound and rules.

Question 8 wants the three major formats: DVI, HDMI, DisplayPort — and every wrong option smuggles in a fake acronym like DMI or HDVI. Read the letters, not the shape of the word.

★ SKILL GET! Native resolution is rows and columns of pixels.

A display's native resolution is the resolution it's optimised for — literally its horizontal rows and vertical columns of pixels, e.g. 1920×1080. Feeding it its native resolution avoids scaling and gives the best picture. Not contrast ratio, not aspect ratio, not sync signals.

📇 VESA and EDID VESA (Video Electronics Standards Association) coordinates computer resolution standards — about 50 of them — and defines EDID, the data by which a display tells connected sources what it can do. Managing EDID gets genuinely hard when several displays run from one source.
★ SKILL GET! Pixels × frames, halve it, triple it.

Computer output bandwidth: [(TP × FR) / 2] × 3 hertz, where TP is total pixels per frame and FR is frame rate. Halve because the worst case is half the pixels white and half black. Triple to leave room for the third harmonic, which keeps signal edges sharp at the display. Resolution up → bandwidth up, directly.

Your textbook's worked example: 1920 × 1080 × 60 = 186.6 MHz. Red, green and blue each carry this.

Lv.8 · producing an image

Through, off, or out 🖥

Displays send light to your eyes in exactly three ways — and the exam asks which category rear projection falls into.

★ SKILL GET! Through, off, or out.

Transmissive — light passes through: rear-screen projection, and LCDs with backlights. Reflective — light bounces off: front-screen projection. Emissive — the display makes its own light and sends it out: LED, OLED, microLED.

transmissive

Rear projection

Good: strong contrast ratio, you can stand at the screen without shadowing, large images possible.

Bad: needs more space than front projection, and hot spotting is possible.

reflective

Front projection

Good: large images, easy to install.

Bad: lower contrast ratio, and the projector sits in the room — so fan and projector noise reach the audience.

emissive

LED · OLED · microLED

Each element creates its own light and sends it straight out. No backlight, no projector.

★ SKILL GET! Light meets the polarizer first.

In an LCD, light must first pass through a polarizer — a set of extremely fine parallel lines acting like a net that blocks every wave not parallel to them. Then it crosses a sandwich of switching transistors and liquid crystals between two polarizing sheets. Because polarizers can't block 100% of the light, LCD "black" is really dark grey.

🚪The screen-door effect.

🖤

Each transistor has to be hidden from the light, so it's covered by a non-transparent layer called the black matrix.

🕸

That matrix draws a hard edge around every pixel — which on lower-resolution panels you see as the "screen door" effect.

📐

The aperture ratio is the see-through part of a pixel divided by the total surface. Smaller transistors → higher aperture ratio → more light and fewer artifacts.

LED & microLED
EMISSIVE · PN JUNCTION
  • A PN junction: electrons cross, and each one emits a photon
  • The materials forming the junction set the wavelength
  • vs LCD: much higher contrast, faster switching, hundreds of thousands of hours, higher brightness, wider gamut
  • Modules link into screens of arbitrary size and shape
  • microLED = 1–10 µm; big gaps between tiny pixels absorb ambient light → deeper blacks
vs
OLED
ORGANIC · CARBON-BASED
  • Organic compounds that emit light when current flows — separate ones for R, G and B
  • Less power than LCD, much higher contrast, possibly higher brightness and wider colour
  • Refreshes in microseconds rather than LCD's milliseconds → superb motion
  • The catch: short lifespan, with progressive output loss — seen as blue fading
★ SKILL GET! First decide what you want to display.

Question 12 asks for the first step in selecting a display type, and the answer isn't mounts, distances or analog-vs-digital — it's determine what you want to display. The content decides how big the image must be, which decides screen size and placement.

aspect ratio

Width over height

Aspect ratio = image width / image height. 4:3 is also 1.33:1; 16:9 is 1.77:1; computer displays are often 16:10.

Both 16/9 (1.7777…) and 4/3 (1.3333…) are repeating decimals — use at least four decimal places in calculations.

Height = width / decimal ratio · Width = height × decimal ratio

⚠ Mount a display in portrait and the ratio reverses — 16:10 becomes 10:16.

positioning

DISCAS

Display size must suit the farthest viewer. Either the display size or that farthest distance can be your starting point.

AVIXA V202.01 (DISCAS) — Display Image Size for 2D Content — gives the calculations for image size, closest and farthest viewers, horizontal angles of view and content design metrics. There's an online calculator at avixa.org.

Lv.9 · the projected image

Optics, lamps and mirrors 📽

Projection uses additive light mixing. And remember: black is simply the absence of projected image — the screen can never get darker than it is with the projector switched off.

★ SKILL GET! It's reFRACTION, not reFLECTION — light goes THROUGH a lens.

Question 7 lists the four primary-optic factors and hides a one-letter trap in three of the four options. The answer is Refraction · Dispersion · Spherical aberration · Curvature of field. A lens bends light through itself; it doesn't bounce it back.

factor 1

Refraction

Light bending as it crosses a boundary between materials — glass to air. How much it bends is the refractive index, and the indexes of a lens's materials affect image quality.

factor 2

Dispersion

Different wavelengths refract at different angles — a prism splitting white light. Strong dispersion gives colour fringes, known as chromatic aberration.

factor 3

Spherical aberration

Light through the edge of a spherical lens leaves at a different angle than light through the centre, giving it a different focal length. The resulting distortion is spherical aberration.

factor 4

Curvature of field

A curved lens projects a curved image field onto a flat screen — so the edges blur while the centre is sharp, or the reverse. Worse with steeply curved, very short focal length (wide-angle) lenses close to the screen.

lamp-based

Three lamp technologies 🔥

  • Tungsten halogen (quartz halogen / QI) — current through tungsten wire until white hot, halogen gas stops blackening. Cheap, but low output, inefficient, short life
  • Metal halide discharge — current between tungsten electrodes in high-pressure metal-halide gas. Much more efficient, much longer life
  • Xenon arc — arc through extremely high-pressure xenon. Very efficient, very high output, but the pressure makes them hazardous to handle

The lamp works with a reflector, internal or external, to collect and direct light.

solid-state

LED & laser 💚

A mix of LEDs, diode lasers and phosphors excited by LEDs or lasers. Some use RGB LEDs for white; RGB laser systems diffuse red, green and blue laser beams.

Energy efficient, lives in the tens of thousands of hours — sometimes approaching the whole operational life of the projector.

Three ways to form the image

3LCD

Light-blocking

Same principle as an LCD panel. Light is split into red, green and blue, each through its own LCD panel, then optically recombined into a full-colour image.

DLP

Thousands of mirrors 🪞

A digital micromirror device (DMD) — microscopic mirrors tilting to reflect light through the lens (on) or into an absorber (off).

Only two states, so shades of grey come from pulse-width modulation (PWM): the ratio of on to off cycles. Too fast for your eye to see flicker.

3DLP = one DMD per colour, combined. Single-chip DLP = one DMD lit alternately through a fast spinning colour wheel, mixed by your persistence of vision.

direct laser

Drawing the picture

A scanning mirror draws the image directly on the surface using collimated narrow-beam lasers in each primary colour, line by line — like the raster on an old CRT.

Front projection
REFLECTIVE · SAME SIDE
  • Reflects both the wanted image and unwanted ambient light back at the viewer
  • Less space and simpler equipment placement
  • Projector shares the room → fan noise, and presenters block the beam
  • Ultra-short throw helps — full coverage from under 18 in / 500 mm
vs
Rear projection
TRANSMISSIVE · BEHIND
  • Light passes through a translucent screen toward the viewer
  • Needs a room or cabinet behind; mirrors fold the optical path to save depth
  • No equipment noise, no shadows when the presenter walks past
  • Handles ambient light better than front projection
Lv.10 · screens & perceived quality

The other half of the system 🎞

"Optimum performance from any projection system is dependent equally on the screen and the projector." Screens are passive — they cannot create light, only redirect it.

★ SKILL GET! More gain, less room.

Screen gain is a screen's ability to redirect light into a narrower viewing area, making the image look brighter to viewers sitting on-axis. The higher the gain number, the brighter the image — but the narrower the angle over which you get that brightness. Matte white is the reference surface: gain 1.0, "unity gain", a perfect diffuser scattering light identically in all directions.

🌓 gain fights ambient light too Ambient light rejection generally increases as gain increases. Higher gain makes the angle light arrives at matter more, so off-axis ambient light gets reflected away from the viewer — the screen looks darker and contrast goes up.
front surface

Matte white

Magnesium carbonate or similar. Perfect diffuser, gain 1.0, good colour rendition. Best for data and graphics.

front surface

Matte grey

Reflects less light overall, but produces higher contrast in the dark areas of an image.

front surface

Angularly reflective

Like a textured mirror — light comes back at the same angle it struck, on the other side of the screen's axis. Most screens above gain 5 are this type. Works well for motion video.

rear surface

Diffusion material

Rigid acrylic, glass, or vinyl fabric for portable use, with a diffused/coated/frosted surface. Wide viewing angle but little or no gain; some hot-spotting possible; moderate ambient light rejection.

Rear-projection screens are generally either narrow and bright or wide and dim.

📐 bigger image, dimmer image Pull the projector back to fill a bigger screen and the image gets noticeably darker — that's the inverse square law again. But careful: the fact that screen edges are dimmer than the centre is not the inverse square law. That's a compromise in projector optical design.

Perceived quality & contrast 🎯

★ SKILL GET! Seven, fifteen, fifty, eighty.

The four minimum contrast ratios from ANSI/INFOCOMM 3M-2011, in the order the viewer gets more demanding: Passive 7:1 → Basic decision-making 15:1 → Analytical decision-making 50:1 → Full-motion video 80:1. Contrast remains the fundamental metric of image quality.

🎛 gadget 05 · how much contrast do you need? 15:1

Six steps from system black up to peak white at the required ratio.

viewing angles

30 up, 60 across

BDM: the top of the image should be no more than 30° above the eye level of the closest viewer; viewers within a 60° arc of the far edge of the screen.

ADM: no vertical recommendation — a viewer may need to walk right up to resolve pixel detail. Horizontally, still the 60° arc.

viewing distance

%EH — element height

An element is the smallest discrete unit a viewer must discern — usually a text character, but also a graph intersection or a symbol.

%EH = element height / total image height. A 38-pixel character on a 1080-high display is %EH of 22; a %EH of 5 is 54 pixels. Take heights from lowercase characters, excluding descenders.

ADM uses single-pixel element height, since every pixel must resolve.

Lv.11 · throw, keystone & alignment

Making it land square 🔧

The projector is chosen. Now put it in the right place and tune it.

throw distance

Projector to screen 📏

Minimum projection distance = closest acceptable. Maximum projection distance = farthest that still makes the required image. Offset = how far vertically the projector can sit from the bottom of the screen.

Every projector's formulas differ, and each lens gets its own. Some use screen width, some the diagonal.

Worked example on a 2.44 m wide screen:
max = (5.05 × 2.44) + 0.2 = 12.52 m
min = (3.2 × 2.44) + 0.26 = 8.07 m
offset = 0.03 × 2.44 = 0.07 m

keystone error

The wonky trapezoid ⬛

Caused by the projector being in the wrong place, so part of the screen is farther away and the light spreads over a larger area. Four fixes:

  • Tilt the screen toward the projector
  • Adjust the projector's height
  • Electronic keystone — reduces the number of activated pixels to square it up
  • Optical lens shift — vertical is most common
★ SKILL GET! Lens shift keeps the pixels. Electronic keystone eats them.

That's why optical correction by lens shift is preferable to electronic keystone correction — it doesn't throw any pixels away.

★ SKILL GET! Centering positions it. Clock sizes it. Phase sharpens it.

Centering does not mean centring the image on the screen — it centres the signal onto the imaging device (the LCD panel or DLP chip), shifting one row or column of pixels at a time. Clock is the projector's master timing; adjust it to stretch or shrink the image to fit the fixed matrix. Phase fine-tunes under one pixel — get it wrong and fine detail goes noisy and fuzzy. They interact, so expect to go round again.

🔍 pro tip from the book Professionals use a test pattern with a single-pixel-width outside border — it makes the exact edges of the picture visible so you can see what's falling off the matrix.

💡ANSI lumens — the nine-zone method.

⬜

Project a full-white pattern, focus at maximum aperture, and set zoom lenses to their midpoint.

🔢

Divide the screen into nine equal areas, take an incident light-meter reading at the centre of each, and average the nine.

✖️

Multiply that average by the screen area to get lumens: L = SH × SW × AI — Screen Height × Screen Width × Average Illuminance.

⚠️

Match your units: lux readings need screen area in square metres; foot-candle readings need square feet.

★ SKILL GET! Brightness sets the black. Contrast sets the white.

Backwards from what the words suggest, and worth ten seconds of memorising. Brightness is the black-level control — too high and blacks turn grey; too low and dark detail merges into one shade. Contrast sets the range between lightest and darkest — too low and whites look grey; too high and white detail merges into one shade.

📋 the setting order Start both controls midrange. 1. Adjust contrast until the light-grey blocks are visible, stopping before detail turns all white. 2. Adjust brightness until the dark-grey blocks are distinguishable, stopping before true black turns grey. They interact — expect to repeat. Greyscale is used because equal amounts of red, green and blue form its levels, so adjusting on grey balances all three colours at once.

💡 light & colour

  1. Electromagnetic radiation is categorised by wavelength. Visible light = 380–750 nm; red is long, violet is short.
  2. Incident lands, reflected leaves. Feet are American. Incident/illuminance = lux (SI) & foot-candle (US). Reflected/luminance = nit (SI) & foot-lambert (US).
  3. Candela = luminous intensity (Latin for candle). Lumen = luminous flux, the unit for projector output; the quoted method is ANSI.
  4. A quarter left means three quarters lost. Double the distance → 1/4 intensity → the exam says decreases by 75%. ×3 → 1/9 · ×5 → 1/25.
  5. Hue, Value, Saturation (Munsell). Light adds RGB, paint subtracts RYB; print inks are CMY.
  6. Hot is blue, cool is orange — colour temperature in kelvin, never degrees. CRI 100 means it burns (only incandescence); cameras struggle below 60.

📷 capture & signal

  1. Short lens, wide view. Focal length = lens to sensor when focused. Big number, small hole, deep field — and dim scenes have shallow depth of field.
  2. Shutter regulates how long the imager accepts light and affects only the imager, not the output frame rate. Imagers are CMOS or CCD.
  3. R, G, B, H, V — three colours and two edges, so full-bandwidth RGBHV needs five cables. Switch in the gap (vertical interval) or you crash.
  4. i = interlaced, odd lines then even, at half the vertical rate. p = progressive, one pass. Horizontal scan rate is always the higher frequency.

🔌 connections & displays

  1. DVI, HDMI, DisplayPort are the three majors — HDMI is DVI plus sound and rules (audio, EDID, HDCP, Ethernet). SDI = BNC 75 Ω coax, no content protection. HDBaseT = one Cat5e + 100 W.
  2. Native resolution = horizontal rows × vertical columns of pixels. VESA sets resolutions and defines EDID. Bandwidth = pixels × frames, halve it, triple it → 1920×1080×60 = 186.6 MHz.
  3. Through, off, or out. Transmissive = rear projection + backlit LCD · reflective = front projection · emissive = LED/OLED/microLED. Light meets the polarizer first, and LCD black is really dark grey.
  4. First decide what you want to display. Aspect ratio = width / height; 4:3 = 1.33:1, 16:9 = 1.77:1; portrait reverses it.

📽 projection & setup

  1. It's reFRACTION, not reFLECTION — the four optic factors are refraction, dispersion, spherical aberration, curvature of field. More gain, less room: higher gain = brighter but narrower; matte white = unity gain 1.0.
  2. Seven, fifteen, fifty, eighty — passive / BDM / ADM / full-motion video. Lens shift keeps the pixels. Centering positions, clock sizes, phase sharpens (bad phase = fuzzy detail). L = SH × SW × AI. Brightness sets the black, contrast sets the white.
