Lv.1 · what a control system is for

One button, no manual 🔘

Control systems allow complex AV systems to be operated using simple interfaces. A complex AV system is many individual components, integrated to perform like a single system — and you shouldn't need to know about each piece of equipment to operate the overall system.

Opening time · 07:00
Digi. To open the café I have to switch on fourteen separate things in a specific order and I have written none of them down.
Then I will give you one button.
One button that does fourteen things? What do I need to know about the fourteen things?
Nothing. That is the entire point of my job.
★ SKILL GET! Control systems simplify. They do not automate.

The exam asks it as a fill-in-the-blank and offers Automate right next to Simplify. The book's word is simplify: a control system simplifies the complex individual functions and steps necessary to complete specific tasks, and makes the system more accessible because it doesn't require technical knowledge to operate the AV equipment. Automation is something control systems can do. Simplifying is what they are for.

★ SKILL GET! A press, a step, a degree.

A control system is event-driven: certain events initiate a change, and the event triggers the control system to perform in a predetermined way. The book's three examples are exactly those three — a button being pressed, a person stepping on a pressure mat, and a temperature setting crossing a threshold. A finger, a foot, and a thermometer.

the shopping list of interfaces There are handheld remote controls, switches, rotary knobs, faders, pressure sensors, and touch panels. Six ways in, before you have programmed a single thing. Anna has opinions about exactly one of them.
Lv.2 · functions & programs

Count the verbs 📋

The most examinable idea in the chapter — it takes two of the nine review questions. According to AVIXA best practice, a control system's behaviours are described in a button-by-button document that narrates the events that occur when someone presses a button. Those events are the button's functions and programs.

★ SKILL GET! One verb is a function. A list is a program.

A function is an individual action — select Lights On, the lights switch on. A program is a sequence of several functions executed by pressing a single button. So when the exam asks which option is a function and which needs a program, count the verbs. “Setting volume levels” — one verb, a function. “Powering on the audio amplifier and display” — two devices, two actions, a program.

Functions

Lv.3 · the CPU & distributed intelligence

The brain in the cupboard 🧠

The primary component of a control system is the central processing unit (CPU). It is the “brain” of the entire system — and on the exam, it is where every button press ends up.

★ SKILL GET! Every button talks to the brain.

Asked what a control system interface sends instructions to, the answer is the control system CPU — not the Internet gateway, not a network printer, not a hard drive. The CPU runs a program that accepts inputs or control requests from an operator or other triggers and then controls the individual devices as specified in that program. And the person who writes it has a title: a control system programmer develops the program that steps the CPU through the required functions.

Digi · the control system programmer
Note the job title. Control system programmer. It is in the book. I did not invent it to feel important.
You did print it on a small card, though.
★ SKILL GET! Three names, one-to-many.

Controlling devices in multiple rooms, across a college campus, or even in another part of the world may need multiple interconnected controllers. Those configurations get called distributed intelligence, distributed processing, or edge processing. The book is refreshingly blunt about it: while these three terms have slightly different meanings, control system professionals use them for one-to-many processes. Three labels, one idea — one brain reaching many rooms.

say it out loud Distributed intelligence. Distributed processing. Edge processing. If a question offers you all three as separate answers, be suspicious — in this chapter they are the same shelf.
Lv.4 · interfaces & triggers

The bit Anna touches 👆

Control systems generally require some form of human interface connected to the CPU. The reason is almost funny in its plainness: the CPU can't normally execute a function if it doesn't receive a command.

👆A hand, or something that acts like one

🚪

An interface might be a multibutton panel installed at the entry door of a presentation room, letting the user pick a lighting level on their way in. Also common: advanced graphical user interfaces (GUIs), touch-sensitive screens, and web-based control panels.

⏰

But a hand is no longer required. It is becoming increasingly common for control systems to trigger functions based on internal timers or external sensors — starting a sequence when a visitor steps on a pressure mat, or when they enter the trigger zone on a camera image.

🎯

How you choose between them is one sentence worth remembering: the needs of the user and the type of experience will dictate the appropriate user interface devices. Not the budget, not the brand — the user and the experience.

Lv.5 · bidirectional vs unidirectional

Does it answer back? 🔁

The CPU and the devices connected to it must be able to communicate to execute the desired command sequence, and the available types of communication, or control signals, vary depending on the capabilities of the CPU and the devices it controls. There are exactly two shapes of conversation.

★ SKILL GET! Two directions, one word: feedback.

In bidirectional communication, a CPU sends out an instruction to a device, the device executes the instruction and then replies to the CPU to confirm its action and status. This is called feedback, and it allows the user to know the status of the device at all times. The exam asks it as “communication that allows a return message” and then offers you omnidirectional and multidirectional as decoys. Bi, not omni — there are only two of you in this conversation.

Lv.6 · contact-closure control

Closed is on, open is off 🔌

Six kinds of control system are coming: contact-closure, variable-voltage, infrared, serial data, radio frequency, and networked. This is the first and the simplest. A contact closure is the simplest form of control communication.

★ SKILL GET! Closed is on. Open is off. That is the whole protocol.

As a system output, contact closure communicates with a remote device by opening or closing an electrical contact that allows a current to flow to the device. The book calls it the most basic protocol: On (contacts closed) or Off (contacts open). The exam asks how it provides device operation, and the answer is exactly that — closing or opening an electrical contact. Not increasing power, not reducing wattage, not adding resistance.

★ SKILL GET! A little switch that throws a big one.

Operation typically requires a low-voltage interface such as a mechanical or solid-state relay to allow the low current of the control signal to operate a higher-current circuit on the device. A tiny signal closing a tiny contact, so that a relay can slam a motor's worth of current somewhere else.

Lv.7 · variable-voltage control

Volts times ten 🎚

The variable-voltage control is an analog form of control. Where contact closure has two states, this one has all the states in between: a voltage within a specified range is applied to the control input of a device to adjust a level by a specified ratio of control voltage to device parameter.

★ SKILL GET! Multiply the volts by ten to get the percent.

Analog-controlled light-dimming technology uses this analog method to control lighting. A 0 to 10V DC signal is applied to the dimming system or light fixture, which matches its output by the specified ratio. The book's own worked example: if a 5V DC signal is applied, the dimmer or fixture output would be set to 50 percent of maximum. And as the applied control voltage changes, the level of dimming changes with it — so 0 V is off, 10 V is full, and 3 V is thirty percent. The arithmetic is doing you a favour here; take it.

Lv.8 · infrared (IR) control

Blinded by the light 📺

Infrared control systems use pulses of invisible IR light to send control signals between devices. The transmitter is usually an LED, and the receiver is usually a photo-transistor or similar light-sensitive device. Patterns of pulses corresponding to control instructions do the talking.

★ SKILL GET! IR is blinded by light, not by dark.

This is the trap, and it is a good one. The review asks what can interfere with an IR control and offers shadows and darkness — which sounds right for something built on light, and is completely backwards. As IR receivers are light-sensitive devices, they can sometimes be overwhelmed by the IR light in bright sunshine or confused by the pulsed light from fluorescent and LED light sources. Darkness is the one thing IR does not mind.

📺Cheap, everywhere, and fussy about three things

💸

IR control is inexpensive to manufacture and simple to integrate into many devices. The most common IR controls are the lightweight remote controls used with consumer devices — from heating and cooling equipment to the entire spectrum of media players and displays. In AV systems it earns its place where a control device needs to be both portable and unencumbered by cables.

👁

Limitation one — it has to see you. Because most objects, including walls and people, are opaque to IR light, there must be a clear line of sight between an IR transmitter and its receivers. A person standing up is a wall.

📏

Limitation two — it does not go far. Most IR systems are limited to an effective range of about 9 to 12 meters (30 to 40 feet), although very high-output LED transmitters can push past that. Signal repeaters can also be used, but long IR paths are prone to being blocked, which makes the situation more suitable for a more penetrating radio-frequency communications link.

🤐

Limitation three — it never answers. IR is usually a one-way communication path, meaning that an IR-controlled device has no way to provide feedback on its status or confirm that it has received a command from the control system. Straight back to Lv.5: IR is unidirectional, so you are on faith and cameras.

Lv.9 · serial digital control

Four numbers to own 🔢

Serial digital control is a method of sending control messages between devices using binary digital codes, frequently over a wired connection. The messages consist of a series of “on” and “off” pulses transmitted sequentially to form control codes.

★ SKILL GET! Written in ASCII so a human can read the argument.

Although the codes could be arbitrary sequences of 1s and 0s, it is common to use ASCII-encoded text sequences to make the messages human-readable for testing and debugging. That is the entire reason: so that when it does not work, somebody can see why. Digi considers this the single most civilised sentence in the chapter.

★ SKILL GET! Unbalanced crosses the room. Balanced crosses the campus.

Same lesson as Chapter 4 and Chapter 7, third time of asking. RS-232 uses an unbalanced line and is thus susceptible to external noise, and the noise issue limits the length of cable you can reliably use to about 15 to 20 meters (50 to 65 feet). Both RS-422 and RS-485 are balanced — and both reach 1,200 meters (4,000 feet). Sixty times further, for the price of a second conductor.

★ SKILL GET! 422 has one voice. 485 has thirty-two.

RS-422 is a multidrop protocol that uses only one transmitter (driver) in most applications. RS-485 supports up to 32 transmitting and receiving devices or, in some applications, 256. Same distance, same balanced trick, wildly different party size. And one more to tie a bow on it: the DMX-512 digital lighting control protocol, used universally in production and architectural lighting, operates over an RS-485 serial communications transmission line — so DMX rides on 485.

Lv.10 · radio-frequency (RF) control

Everyone lives at 2.4 📡

Radio frequency control uses wireless signaling via radio waves to communicate between devices. The physics waits until Chapter 10; this level is the applications of RF communication for wireless AV control, and there are three of them.

★ SKILL GET! Zigbee thirty, Bluetooth ten.

Two ranges, both line of sight. Zigbee has a maximum range of about 30 meters (100 feet) line of sight and can use mesh network relays to extend its coverage. Bluetooth is designed for personal area networking over distances up to about 10 meters (30 feet) line of sight. Zigbee is a building; Bluetooth is a handbag.

★ SKILL GET! Everyone lives at 2.4 — so knock before you move in.

Zigbee operates in the unlicensed industrial, scientific, and medical (ISM) 2.4GHz band worldwide (and ISM 900MHz, 780MHz and 860MHz in some other countries). Bluetooth operates in the ISM 2.4GHz band. Wi-Fi is there too. Which is why: when using RF control, you must verify the frequencies that are already in use in the environment you intend to control — and it is almost impossible to guarantee that the required communications channels will be available and free from interference. This can become critical at times when many people are present in the AV space, because every person in the room arrives carrying two more radios.

30 m · 100 ft

📡 Zigbee

Inexpensive, low-energy, low-speed — designed for remote device communication and control over a small area such as a house or an office.

  • Mesh network relays extend coverage
  • ISM 2.4GHz worldwide · 900, 780, 860 MHz elsewhere
  • Domestic appliance control and industrial applications
  • The basis for some implementations of the Internet of Things (IoT)
the one already there

🛜 Wi-Fi

A general data communications protocol used for computer networking — Chapter 6's territory.

  • Chosen because of its wide implementation across most spaces where AV systems are used and the wide availability of devices with integrated Wi-Fi
  • Used for both control communications and data distribution
  • Also used in some IoT implementations
10 m · 30 ft

🦷 Bluetooth

Low-energy, for personal area networking.

  • ISM 2.4GHz band
  • Widely implemented in computing and peripheral devices
  • Point-to-point control and data communications in portable audio equipment
  • Also used in some IoT implementations

Lv.11 · networked control systems

The one that ate the others 🌐

The Ethernet network has become the standard means of communicating between all types of devices, not only for control but also for content. And, the book adds, nowhere is this more so than in the world of AV.

★ SKILL GET! Layer 2 is the exception. Layer 3 is the rule.

While there remain a few proprietary AV applications that run directly over the data link layer (OSI layer 2) of an Ethernet network, the vast majority of Ethernet communications and control applications use the TCP/IP networking protocol (OSI layer 3 and above) as their underlying architecture. And that is what buys you the world: TCP/IP allows the possibility of routing and communications across the entire Internet. Straight back to Chapter 6's ladder — Please Don't Nibble The Sugar.

🌐What the network gets you, and what it costs

🔗

Ethernet allows communication among control components, devices, input systems, output systems, applications, data, and the Internet. One cable type for the control, the content and the company email. Chapter 7's careful signal separation did not see this coming.

📶

Wi-Fi has effectively become the unwired component of Ethernet networks. That is the book's phrasing and it is worth keeping — Wi-Fi is not a rival to Ethernet here, it is Ethernet with the wire taken out.

🤝

The cost is political, not technical. Placing control systems on networks requires considerable advanced planning and coordination between AV professionals and information technology (IT) departments. Somebody owns that switch, and it is not you.

🏢

What it unlocks: real-time room support, loss prevention, inventory management, facility usage and scheduling, and web-centric presentation environments. And so the control scenario has shifted from the needs of one facility to enterprise-wide considerations — from this room to every room we own.

The cheat sheet 📋 ✧

Three blocks, twelve lines.

🔘 what it is

  1. Control systems SIMPLIFY the operation of an AV system — not automate, not reconfigure. They let complex AV systems be operated using simple interfaces, and you shouldn't need to know about each piece of equipment.
  2. Event-driven: a press, a step, a degree. A button being pressed, a person stepping on a pressure mat, a temperature crossing a threshold — the event triggers the control system to perform in a predetermined way.
  3. One verb is a function. A list is a program. A function is an individual action (setting volume levels); a program is a sequence of several functions executed by pressing a single button (powering on the amplifier and display). AVIXA documents it button by button.
  4. Every button talks to the brain — the interface sends instructions to the control system CPU. A control system programmer writes the program. Distributed intelligence / distributed processing / edge processing = three names for one-to-many. Valid interfaces: GUIs, touch panels, wall switches — not flip charts.

🔌 the wired ways to talk to a box

  1. Bi, not omni — two directions, one word: feedback. Bidirectional: the device replies to confirm its action and status, so you know the status at all times. Unidirectional: no return acknowledgment, so other methods such as surveillance cameras may be required.
  2. Closed is on, open is off. Contact closure is the simplest form, operating a device by closing or opening an electrical contact, usually through a mechanical or solid-state relay so a small current can switch a big one. Drives motors, screens, drapes, shades, HVAC, power feeds — and works as an input too (occupancy, panel position, broken light beam, pressure mat, audio over-level).
  3. Volts times ten is percent. Variable voltage is analog: a 0 to 10V DC signal sets output by a specified ratio — 5V DC = 50 percent of maximum.
  4. IR is blinded by light, not by dark. Bright sunshine and pulsed light from fluorescent and LED sources break it; walls and people are opaque to IR so it needs clear line of sight; range 9 to 12 m (30 to 40 ft); and it is usually one-way, with no feedback. Transmitter = LED, receiver = photo-transistor.
  5. Unbalanced crosses the room, balanced crosses the campus. Serial digital sends binary codes, usually as ASCII text so they are human-readable for testing and debugging. USB < 5 m (16 ft) · RS-232 15–20 m (50–65 ft), unbalanced, point-to-point, DB9 · RS-422 1,200 m (4,000 ft), balanced four-wire, one transmitter · RS-485 1,200 m (4,000 ft), balanced, 32 devices (or 256), UTP, and DMX-512 rides on it.

📡 the wireless & networked ways

  1. Zigbee thirty, Bluetooth ten. Zigbee: low-energy, low-speed, 30 m (100 ft) line of sight, mesh relays extend it, ISM 2.4GHz worldwide, a basis for IoT. Bluetooth: personal area networking, 10 m (30 ft), ISM 2.4GHz, point-to-point. Wi-Fi: used for both control and data distribution because it is already everywhere.
  2. Everyone lives at 2.4, so knock before you move in. You must verify the frequencies already in use, and it is almost impossible to guarantee a free channel — critical when many people are present.
  3. Layer 2 is the exception, layer 3 is the rule. A few proprietary AV apps run on the data link layer (OSI 2); the vast majority use TCP/IP (OSI 3 and above), which allows routing across the entire Internet. Wi-Fi is the unwired component of Ethernet. Ethernet is mainly there to control complex systems, and it needs advance planning and coordination with IT. Choose a control system on what you need to control, where, and who is using it.
